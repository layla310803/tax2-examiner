This question consists of two parts
Part A
ClutterClear Solutions (Pty) Ltd (‘ClutterClear’ or ‘the company’) is a professional home organisation
company that specialises in decluttering and optimising spaces for both residential and commercial
clients. It is a South African company and a registered category B VAT vendor. The company was
founded in 2015 by two friends, Amanda Ngidi (age 45) and Tshifhiwa Malange (age 47), who both
have a passion for creating functional, aesthetically pleasing environments. Amanda and Tshifhiwa
each hold 50% of the shares in the company.
The company offers a range of services including personalised consultations, hands-on organising
and the sale of customised storage solutions. With a growing client base and a reputation for
transforming untidy spaces into orderly, stress-free environments, the company has expanded its
operations nationally.
The company has a financial year ending 31 December and has already calculated its taxable income
for the current year of assessment to be R263 000 and its taxable supplies to be R550 000. However,
it has a number of outstanding transactions detailed below for finalisation and advice thereon which
have not yet been taken into account in the calculation of the taxable income and taxable supplies
above.
Recall and rebranding
During the current year of assessment, the company launched a new line of branded storage
containers. However, due to a labeling error, the containers were branded with incorrect weight
capacity limits. This mistake posed a risk to customers, as the containers could be overloaded and
potentially be broken. To mitigate the risk, the company decided to recall the faulty containers and
replace them with correctly labeled ones. The recall and rebranding came at a significant cost of
R1 300 000 to the company during the current year of assessment, which included costs such as
printing new labels, shipping the recalled containers back to the warehouse, disposal of the faulty
containers and replacing the faulty containers.
Theft of motor vehicle
On 1 August 2024, one of the delivery vehicles was stolen while parked outside after making a
delivery. It was a single-cab delivery vehicle that was purchased on 1 July 2022. It was purchased
new at a cost of R362 900 when the open market value was R360 000. The single-cab bakkie was
insured and the insurance paid out an amount of R365 000. While this amount was used to purchase
a new double-cab light delivery vehicle, the company had to pay in extra as the cost of the double-
cab light delivery vehicle was R400 000 (which was equal to the open market value). The new bakkie
was bought and brought into use on 1 September 2024.
Services provided to shareholders
Amanda and Tshifhiwa have never used the services of the company in their own homes as they
were always able to keep their own homes organised and decluttered. However, Amanda has moved
homes and does not have time to do all the organisation herself. She has used the services of the
company. Tshifhiwa has said that the company will do it at no charge. However, Amanda wants to
pay to avoid any negative tax consequences. She paid R10 000 for the service, when it would usually

Tshifhiwa Malange recently heard on the radio that it was “The Wills Week Campaign” in South
Africa where free will drafting services are offered to the public to ensure that individuals can
safeguard their estate and ensure that their assets are properly managed and distributed after
death.
Tshifhiwa has been married to her husband out of community of property since 2002, and they have
two children, aged 20 and 17, so she wanted to make sure that if anything happens to her, her
family will be taken care of. She decided to make an appointment to draft her will and also get some
advice on any estate planning that she should consider.
After meeting with the consultant, she decided to donate some of her assets to her family. On 1 July
2024, Tshifhiwa made the following donations. Prior to this, she has never donated anything before.
 She donated her Toyota Corolla to her oldest child so that he had a car to get to university
and not have to rely on her or her husband to drop him off. She had bought the car in 2019
for R350 000 and the car had a market value of R200 000 on the date of the donation.
 She donated an apartment situated in Zimbabwe to her children in equal shares. She had
inherited the apartment from her late father (who was a resident of Zimbabwe) when he
passed away in 2005. He had purchased the apartment in 2002 for R1 300 000. When she
inherited it, the apartment had a market value of R1 500 000 (correctly translated) and at the
date that she donated it, the value had increased to R3 200 000.
 She donated her old laptop that she used for personal emails and watching series to a local
orphanage, a registered Public Benefit Organisation. She bought the laptop for R12 500 in
2020 and at the date of the donation it was worth R8 000.
 She donated her collection of Kruger Rands worth R450 000 which she bought in 2014 for
R340 000 to her mother.
On 15 October 2024, Tshifhiwa was involved in a fatal accident on her way home from work. The
following information relates to her estate:
 Amanda Ngidi had taken out an insurance policy on Tshifhiwa’s life so that, in the event of
her death, she could buy out Tshifhiwa’s shares in ClutterClear (Pty) Ltd. The policy paid out
R1 000 000 to Amanda. Amanda had made all the contributions of R300 000 (inclusive of
interest at 6% of R54 455) towards this policy and Tshifhiwa had never made any
contributions.
 The shares in ClutterClear (Pty) Ltd were to be sold to Amanda during the course of
liquidation of the deceased estate. The shares had a market value of R1 800 000 at death
and were sold to Amanda for R1 600 000.
 Tshifhiwa and her husband took out a domestic life policy after entering into their marriage.
The policy on her life paid out an amount of R3 000 000 to her husband after her death. Her
husband paid all the premiums relating to this policy, amounting to R500 000. Interest
calculated on the premiums at 6% amounted to R81 417.
 Her primary residence, that she and her family had been living in for the past few years
which was in Claremont Cape Town, was left to her husband. Tshifhiwa bought the house in
2012 for R2 500 000 and at the date of her death it was valued at R5 600 000.
 Her holiday home in Langebaan, Western Cape, was to be sold and the proceeds split
equally between her two children. Tshifhiwa bought the house in 2017 for R3 200 000 and at
the date of her death it was valued at R4 200 000. The executor sold it for R2 500 000 in the
course of liquidation of her estate.
